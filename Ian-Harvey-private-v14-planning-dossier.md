## 5. Technical Critique and Self-Consistency Review

### 5.1 Strong Points To Preserve From v10.4

v10.4 is the correct foundation. It has the desired simulator look, uses the full 1977 context in key views, preserves uncertainty bands, and has the RC/Tarboton comparison framework Ian likes. Its forecast baseline begins at about 4192.1 ft NGVD29, which agrees with the live USGS reading on 2026-05-21.

The v10.4 charting system is also better structured. Policy sliders visibly affect the RC forecast, Tarboton bands respond to total added kAf, and the combined model comparison already exists in a polished form.

### 5.2 Problems To Correct From v10.4

The old ENSO implementation should not be carried forward as-is. In v10.4, ENSO modifies the effective groundwater time constant:

```text
tau_eff = tau_slow_base * (1 - 0.1 * ENSO)
```

That is a compact modeling shortcut, but the new documents argue that ENSO acts primarily through atmospheric-river event frequency and episodic recharge, with PDO shaping the bridge geometry. In v14, ENSO should be a scenario forcing on AR/storm delivery and a stochastic/event pathway, not only a time-constant knob.

v10.4 also treats all policy levers as a summed annual kAf contribution with a generic ramp. That is no longer adequate. v14 needs per-year delivery arrays or editable tables for each lever, with lever-specific persistence rules.

The existing v10.4 forecast ends in 2034. v14 should extend selected views through 2036, but not pretend 2036 point estimates are high-skill. The cone should visibly widen.

### 5.3 Problems To Reject Or Repair From v12

v12's code confirms Ian's concern that sliders do not really drive the Overview graph. In `updateSimple()`, the code first converts kAf to feet using `FPK`, then applies `FPK` again when updating the chart series. This double conversion makes the graph nearly insensitive to the sliders.

v12's year-by-year accounting tables are mostly explanatory. The simulator trajectory uses the current slider values summed across levers, not the annual table values. Editing or opening a year table does not drive downstream model state.

v12 hard-codes:

```text
BASE = 4187.5
```

That conflicts with v10.4 and with live USGS Saltair data near 4192.1 ft NGVD29. v14 should not inherit this baseline.

v12 drops several v10.4 tabs Ian explicitly wants preserved. It is therefore a concept source, not a structural source.

v12 labels hypothesis content as Cross-Validation. In v14, reserve Cross-Validation for model comparison/water-budget closure and move hypothesis tests into a Testing Hypotheses tab.

### 5.4 Technical Assertions That Need Qualification

1. PDO at -4 sigma: Supported for July 2025 in the NCEI file, not for the latest complete 2026 NCEI months. Use "recently reached extreme negative values in 2025 and remained negative in early 2026" unless a newer official source confirms -4 again.
2. AR multipliers x1.8 and x3.2: Useful as provisional conceptual coefficients, but not calibrated enough for unqualified scientific claims. Label provisional until Chikamoto or an AR catalog analysis supplies coefficients.
3. 6-year vs 8-year vs 9.3-year lag: These are not necessarily contradictions, but the paper must define what each measures. The safest framing is:
   - 3 years: precipitation anomaly to GSL elevation response.
   - 6 years: QDO/PDO to precipitation to GSL phase chain in published lag framing.
   - 7 to 8 years: cross-correlation/regression optimum depending on variable and method.
   - 9.3 years: RC transfer-function phase of the full modeled cascade.
4. "PDO forcing": Avoid language that treats PDO as a literal independent forcing. Use "regime indicator" or "index of atmospheric bridge geometry."
5. 2030-2031 bump: It is a good self-consistency test, but it should be presented as an expected lag-window response with uncertainty, not as a requirement that the model must force into existence.

## 6. Updated RC White Paper Draft For v14 Embedding

### Title

Great Salt Lake Water Level Forecasting via RC Circuit Analogy: Lag-Corrected PDO, ENSO, and Atmospheric-River Extension

### Author

Ian R. Harvey

### Status

Private working draft for v14 simulator embedding. Prepared from v10.4 white paper plus May 2026 addenda. Coauthor names intentionally omitted until participation is separately invited.

### Executive Summary

The Great Salt Lake can be modeled as a forced, damped hydrologic oscillator whose watershed behaves like a multi-stage RC circuit. Water volume is analogous to charge, lake elevation to voltage, inflow to current, basin storage to capacitance, and evaporation/diversions to resistance. The model's practical value comes from separating three signals that are often conflated:

1. The long structural decline caused by aridification, diversions, and reduced recovery capacity.
2. The quasi-decadal Pacific regime signal indexed by PDO/QDO, which appears after multi-year hydrologic routing.
3. Episodic ENSO-related atmospheric-river events, whose precipitation effects can arrive quickly but whose lake-elevation response is delayed.

The v14 update preserves the v10.4 RC model and Tarboton comparison framework while correcting the lag logic, updating the snow-fraction anchor, and adding a policy accounting structure that reflects year-specific water availability.

### Core Model Framing

The watershed is modeled as a low-pass hydrologic filter. Surface routing, soil moisture, reservoir storage, groundwater, and lake integration do not transmit climate forcing instantly. The RC analogy is useful because it encodes both attenuation and delay:

```text
External climate regime -> precipitation/storm delivery -> surface routing
                         -> soil moisture and groundwater storage
                         -> baseflow and lake inflow -> GSL elevation
```

The PDO should not be described as a direct physical actuator on the lake. It is better treated as an index that tracks a broader Pacific climate regime and atmospheric bridge geometry. Its value is that it correlates with the low-frequency forcing band that the GSL watershed actually transmits.

### Lag Accounting

The apparent disagreement between 6-year, 7-year, 8-year, and 9.3-year lags is resolved by recognizing that each number belongs to a different measurement frame.

| Lag number | Meaning | Use in v14 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 years | Approximate precipitation-to-GSL elevation response | Display as the public-facing "snowpack now, lake later" lag |
| 3 + 3 = 6 years | Pacific QDO/PDO to precipitation plus precipitation to GSL chain | Use in explanatory text and figure captions |
| 7.2 +/- 1.5 years | Spectral coherence phase estimate | Use in technical white paper |
| 7 to 8 years | Cross-correlation/regression peak depending on variable | Use for statistical validation and model fitting |
| 9.3 years | Full RC transfer-function phase lag | Use as the complete-cascade model parameter, with caveat |

This framing lets the simulator use an 8-ish-year Pacific foresight window without implying that every hydrologic sub-process takes eight years.

### ENSO, PDO, and Atmospheric Rivers

The v10.4 model largely excluded ENSO or treated it as a time-constant modifier. The v14 framework should instead represent ENSO through atmospheric-river delivery and event probability. El Nino can increase the probability of large precipitation events, but whether those events efficiently reach the Great Basin depends on atmospheric bridge geometry, which PDO helps index.

The working hypothesis:

```text
PDO phase governs decadal recharge capacity.
ENSO affects episodic recharge magnitude through atmospheric-river frequency and intensity.
PDO phase can amplify or suppress the ENSO-to-AR pathway.
```

Current official data as of May 2026 support a high probability of El Nino onset but do not yet guarantee a very strong coupled event. Official PDO data support a negative/cool PDO background through early 2026, with extreme negative values observed in 2025. Therefore v14 should model 2026-27 as "El Nino developing into a cool PDO background" rather than a simple repeat of 2022-23.

### Two-Stage Lag Cascade

For public and policy communication, the key distinction is:

| Stage | Visible effect | Expected timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stage 1 | Precipitation, snowpack, streamflow, reservoir storage | Same water year to about +1 year |
| Stage 2 | GSL elevation response after routing/storage | About +3 years after precipitation anomaly |

Thus a 2026-27 wet signal can be real while the lake does not visibly respond until about 2029-2031. v14 should make that lag visible instead of hiding it inside a single forecast line.

### Snow Fraction Correction

The snow-fraction anchor should be corrected. The 84% value belongs to 1960, not 1980. Using the reported decline of about 0.18 percentage points per year:

| Year | Corrected snow fraction |
| --- | --- |
| 1960 | 84.0% |
| 1980 | About 80.4% |
| 2010 | About 75.0% |
| 2025 | About 72% |

This matters because the character of precipitation is changing. Rain-dominant or pulse-dominant delivery has lower capture efficiency than slower snowmelt-dominated recharge. v14 should treat declining snow fraction as an efficiency penalty or routing/capture modifier, not merely a climate footnote.

### Policy Lever Accounting

Each lever should have annual delivery values from 2026 through 2036. These values should drive the model. Do not treat the tables as explanatory decoration.

Recommended lever accounting types:

| Lever type | Examples | Modeling rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stable recurring | M&I conservation, US Magnesium cessation if operations remain ceased | Benefit persists each year unless explicitly changed |
| Wet-year dependent | Ag leasing, reservoir operations, cloud seeding | Benefit is conditional on prior/current water-year state |
| One-time stock transfer | Newfoundland ponds | Delivery occurs once, then must recharge/refill before recurring |
| Buildout trajectory | Phragmites, infrastructure, funded acquisitions | Benefit ramps over years and then requires maintenance |

The simulator should expose annual values directly and recompute forecast outputs whenever a year value changes.

### Validation and Self-Criticism

The model should keep the v10.4 self-criticism posture. Main limitations:

1. Long-range forecast skill is bounded by PDO predictability.
2. AR multipliers are provisional until calibrated against a stratified AR catalog.
3. Stationarity may fail under warming.
4. Snow fraction and rain-on-snow changes may alter routing efficiency.
5. Policy delivery is institutional and hydrologic, not merely arithmetic.
6. A 2036 forecast should be treated as a widening probability distribution, not a point prediction.
