Damped Oscillator Signature in GSL Volume

The raw correlation between the Pacific Decadal Oscillation index and Great Salt Lake volume change is modest (R ≈ 0.15). However, when we isolate the decadal oscillation through bandpass filtering (retaining only the 8–25 year periodicity), the coherence between Pacific SST patterns and GSL ΔV is strong — confirming that there is a real teleconnection signal buried under the noise of interannual weather variability, ENSO events, and management decisions. The RC circuit model exploits this filtered coherence, which is why its forecast skill exceeds what the raw correlation would suggest.

Statistical Foundation: PDO–GSL Teleconnection

Key result (p < 0.001): The teleconnection between Pacific SST patterns and Great Salt Lake volume is statistically significant at the 99.9% confidence level. The raw Pearson correlation coefficient (r) at the optimal 8-year lag is modest (~0.15), but this is expected — raw correlation measures linear association across all frequencies, including interannual noise from ENSO events, local weather, and management decisions that obscure the decadal signal.

Bandpass-filtered coherence (8–25 yr periods): strong. When we isolate the decadal oscillation band through bandpass filtering — retaining only the 8–25 year periodicities — the correlation rises dramatically (see Glossary for statistical terms). This confirms a real physical teleconnection at decadal timescales, which is exactly the frequency band the RC circuit model is designed to exploit.

Why 8 Years?

Cumulative delay through Wang’s physical chain: PDO regime → storm track shifts (months) → snowpack (1 yr) → soil moisture (1–2 yr) → groundwater baseflow (3–5 yr) = total ~8 years.

Climate Change Depression: The RC model captures warming-driven increases in evapotranspiration through two mechanisms: (1) the exponentially decaying amplitude envelope, which implicitly reflects both anthropogenic water diversion and increasing evaporative demand; and (2) the v10.4 real-time climate adjustment, which applies an explicit year-by-year correction when current ET₀ conditions deviate from the long-term structural baseline.

How the Great Salt Lake Simulator Works

Normally we see graphs of lake elevation changing over time. Here we convert elevation to lake volume (blue line) using USGS data relating elevation to volume based on measured surface area and measured shape of the lake basin. Then by plotting the annual change in volume as positive (green) or negative (pink) values, we have a measured annual water budget indicating whether inflows from streams dominate the volume change or whether we do not have enough inflow to offset the evaporation rate.

Full Record (1930–2025) — Modern Damping Behavior Highlighted

The full record reveals a critical transition around ~1977 when the PDO underwent a well-documented regime shift. Prior to 1977, the lake oscillated naturally with large, irregular swings. After 1977, periodic oscillations representative of external forcing suddenly manifest — the lake was no longer its own resilient master. The post-1977 exponential damping envelope (dashed lines) quantifies this structural collapse.

It was the strong periodicity of this graph (and the expanded view on the Damped Oscillator tab) that were the primary clues that standard signal processing approaches could be used to analyze the Lake’s behavior and diagnose what amounts to the Lake’s death throes now as different from when our grandparents or uncles watched the lake go through dry cycles in 1935 and 1962. It is different this time, both in the diagnosis and in the ability to forecast, because 150 years of stream diversions have removed Great Salt Lake’s resilience to natural variation; and because the periodicity we see today links to complex atmospheric and surface sea temperatures that occurred in the Pacific 8–10 years ago. This is an advantage to us (one among very few) because if that scientifically determined “teleconnection” holds, it means that we can link what is happening TODAY in the Pacific with what will likely be occurring (wet or dry periods; impact on lake elevation) in the forecast period that includes the 2034 Olympics. It says: if we do nothing, the world will come to Utah to ski on rocks.

⚠️ Why Forecasting Matters

Great Salt Lake is in a megadrought — the worst sustained dry period in at least 1,200 years (Williams et al., 2022). Annual inflows fall short of combined evaporation and diversions by approximately 800 kAf/yr (GSL Strike Team 2026). Without intervention, elevation will continue declining toward the ecological danger threshold of 4,189 ft, where salinity exceeds 17% and the brine shrimp food web collapses.

However, the lake’s decline is not random. Beneath the drought, the volume record contains quasi-periodic oscillations coherent with Pacific climate patterns on 5–20 year timescales. These oscillations encode predictability — and predictability is what this simulator exploits. It is one of the very few advantages we have in managing the Lake’s uncertain future.

Climate Framing: The Pacific-Basin Multi-Scale Dynamics

A critical framing contribution comes from S.-Y. Simon Wang (2026), who cautioned against treating the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) as an independent external forcing function. Instead, the RC model recognizes the PDO as a convenient “voltmeter” that integrates a complex chain of tropical–extratropical coupled dynamics.

Rather than a standalone driver, the model captures the broad climate variability commonly embedded in the PDO index, which is more accurately viewed as the collective manifestation of:

  • The Tropical Pacemaker: Low-frequency shifts in the tropical Pacific (e.g., ENSO, the Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation, even Atlantic climate modes) that regulate global moisture availability.
  • The Atmospheric Bridge: The physical “piping” (such as the Pacific–North American teleconnection) that steers moisture flux and storm tracks toward the Great Basin watershed.
  • The Integrated Response: The chain from tropical circulation to basin soil moisture and groundwater storage naturally produces multi-year lags — approximately 9.3 years in this model — that are “very RC-like” in behavior.

The PDO as an Integrating Indicator

This framing is essential: the RC model does not treat the PDO as a deterministic driver, despite its strong spectral coherence in the 15–25 year band confirming that the index effectively integrates the multi-scale dynamics of the Pacific moisture delivery system. The model’s 8-year lag parameter (τlag) thus represents the physical time required for the watershed’s capacitance to filter and respond to these broad, decadal-scale climate regimes.

Note: the corridor of decadal Pacific influences runs from northern California to Great Salt Lake. See Stuivenvolt-Allen et al. (2021) and Stuivenvolt Allen et al. (2021) for the atmospheric river and water availability connections.

🔌 The RC Circuit Analogue

The GSL watershed is modeled as a multi-stage RC (resistor-capacitor) circuit where:

  • Water volume = Electric charge (kAf ↔ Coulombs)
  • Elevation = Voltage (feet ↔ Volts)
  • Flow rate = Current (kAf/yr ↔ Amperes)
  • Storage capacity = Capacitance (kAf/ft ↔ Farads)
  • Evaporation + Diversions = Resistance (leakage)
  • PDO-driven precipitation = AC voltage source (~10-yr period)

Three parallel tributary cascades (Bear 60%, Weber 25%, Jordan 15%) feed GSL as the final integrating capacitor (τGSL = 20 years). Policy levers inject additional current. Lake-effect precipitation creates a positive feedback loop.

📊 Quasi-Periodic Oscillation Forecasting

The RC model’s forecasting power comes from a well-documented physical mechanism: Pacific Ocean sea-surface temperature patterns organize into quasi-periodic oscillations (5–20 year timescales) that propagate through atmospheric teleconnections to affect Great Basin hydrology with a cumulative lag of ~8 years. By treating the watershed as an electrical circuit driven by this quasi-periodic signal, the model converts climate regime observations into actionable hydrologic forecasts. This is not a statistical correlation exercise — it is a physics-based transfer function exploiting a documented teleconnection pathway.

How the Baseline Is Computed

12 numbers that anchor everything:
1. τGSL = 20 yr (lake integration time, Huybers 2015)
2. τfast = 1.5 yr (surface routing timescale)
3. τslow = 20 yr (groundwater, Brooks et al. 2021)
4. α = 0.41 (runoff partition, Brooks coupling)
5. β = 2.75 (GW/surface coupling parameter)
6. E+D = 800 kAf/yr (base evaporation + diversions)
7. Total phase lag = ~8 years (computed cross-correlation)
8. Yeager LEP coeff = 0.08125 kAf/sq mi
9. Baseline inflow = 1,665 kAf/yr (Strike Team 2026)
10. Danger threshold = 4,189 ft NGVD29
11. Healthy target = 4,198 ft NGVD29 (Strike Team)
12. PDO forecast skill = ~60% at 5 yr (Wang et al., 2010)
All calibrated against USGS NGVD29 gage data.

v10.4 Real-Time Climate Adjustment: When enabled, the model fetches year-to-date ET₀ observations from Open-Meteo (ECMWF ERA5-Land) and compares them against a month-by-month ET₀ climatology (2019–2024 ERA5-Land normals). The ratio of observed-to-expected cumulative ET₀ yields a real-time evaporation anomaly applied to the current water year. This corrects for abnormally hot/dry or cool/wet conditions that deviate from the long-term structural baseline.

Testing Hypotheses: Lag-Corrected Statistical Framing

This tab preserves the useful v12 hypothesis material without replacing the v10.4 Cross-Validation tab. It is a private-draft interpretation layer for the PDO/ENSO/AR addendum.

H1 — Structural Aridification Trend

Supported. GSL elevation is declining at approximately −0.33 ft/yr over the modern record, with a strong negative trend and very small p-value. This is the baseline structural finding, not a scenario assumption.

H2 — PDO as Decadal Regime Indicator

Supported at decadal scale; null at annual scale. PDO should be treated as a regime indicator for atmospheric bridge geometry, not a literal annual forcing knob. The useful signal appears in rolling/lagged decadal framing.

H3 — ENSO via Atmospheric Rivers

AR pathway supported; annual SWE correlation remains weak. ENSO likely matters through AR frequency and intensity, not through a simple annual snowpack mean. This is the mechanism requiring AR-catalog calibration.

H4 — PDO Suppression Paradox

2026 is not a clean 2022–23 analog. PDO reached −4.21 in July 2025 and remains cool/negative in the latest official 2026 data. AR multipliers x1.8 and x3.2 are provisional until Chikamoto/AR-catalog calibration.

⚠ Datum Note: NGVD29 vs NAVD88

This simulator uses NGVD29 (National Geodetic Vertical Datum of 1929) throughout, because it produces exact agreement with the GSL Strike Team’s water budget analysis and the USGS Saltair gage historical record. A newer datum, NAVD88 (North American Vertical Datum of 1988), is offset by approximately −3.18 ft at the Saltair gauge location.

Conversion: NAVD88 ≈ NGVD29 − 3.18 ft

Policymakers, media, and some recent USGS publications may report in either datum. Always verify which datum is being used when comparing numbers. The slider below displays both systems simultaneously.

1. Introduction

The Great Salt Lake (GSL) faces a structural water deficit in which annual inflows fall short of combined evaporation and diversions by approximately 800 kAf/yr. This white paper describes the complete methodology behind the GSL Water Management Simulator, an interactive forecasting tool built on electrical circuit theory.

The v14 update preserves the v10.4 RC model and Tarboton comparison framework while adding lag-corrected PDO/ENSO/atmospheric-river interpretation, year-specific policy accounting, live-USGS forecast anchoring, and explicit treatment of structural baseline gains already realized.

1.2 Climate Framing: The Pacific-Basin Multi-Scale Dynamics

A critical framing contribution comes from S.-Y. Simon Wang (2026), who cautioned against treating the PDO as an independent external forcing function. Instead, the RC model recognizes the PDO as a convenient “voltmeter” that integrates a complex chain of tropical–extratropical coupled dynamics.

Rather than a standalone driver, the model captures the broad climate variability commonly embedded in the PDO index, which is more accurately viewed as the collective manifestation of: (1) The Tropical Pacemaker — low-frequency shifts in the tropical Pacific that regulate global moisture availability; (2) The Atmospheric Bridge — the physical “piping” (Pacific–North American teleconnection) that steers moisture flux toward the Great Basin; (3) The Integrated Response — the chain from tropical circulation to basin soil moisture and groundwater storage naturally produces multi-year lags (~9.3 years) that are “very RC-like.”

The corridor of decadal Pacific influences runs from northern CA to GSL. See Stuivenvolt-Allen et al. (2021, JGR Atmospheres) and Stuivenvolt Allen et al. (2021, Water).

1.3 Current PDO and ENSO Framing for v14

Official NOAA/NCEI PDO data show that PDO reached −4.21 in July 2025 and remained cool/negative in the latest official 2026 data available for this private draft. Therefore v14 describes the near-term climate state as developing El Niño into a cool PDO background, not as a simple repeat of the 2022–23 compound recharge event.

The atmospheric-river multipliers used in the supporting notes (x1.8 for a suppressed 2026-style case and x3.2 for a stronger 2022–23-style analog) are treated as provisional coefficients pending Chikamoto review and calibration against a stratified atmospheric-river catalog.

7. ENSO, PDO, and Atmospheric-River Extension

The v10.4 simulator represented ENSO as a compact adjustment to the effective groundwater time constant. The v14 interpretation keeps that mechanism as a scenario knob, but reframes the physical pathway: ENSO primarily changes the probability and intensity of atmospheric-river recharge events, while PDO phase indexes the decadal atmospheric bridge geometry that can amplify or suppress whether those events efficiently reach the Great Basin.

For policy communication, the lag cascade is split into two visible stages. Stage 1 is the precipitation/streamflow/reservoir signal in the same water year to about +1 year. Stage 2 is the lake-elevation response after watershed routing and storage, approximately +3 years later. Thus a 2026–27 wet signal can be real while GSL elevation does not show the corresponding response until roughly 2029–2031.

📚 Glossary of Terms

Key terms used throughout the simulator, the RC Circuit White Paper, and GSL policy discussions.

Units & Measurements

AF (Acre-Foot)
Volume of water covering one acre one foot deep. Equals 325,851 gallons or ~1,233 m³. Roughly enough to supply two households for one year.
kAf (Thousand Acre-Feet)
1,000 AF. The standard unit for GSL water budget discussions. Fills about 500 Olympic swimming pools.
MAf (Million Acre-Feet)
1,000,000 AF. GSL volume ranges from roughly 15–22 MAf depending on elevation. One MAf could supply greater Salt Lake City for approximately five years.
NGVD29
National Geodetic Vertical Datum of 1929. The elevation reference used throughout this simulator and by the USGS Saltair gage historical record.
NAVD88
North American Vertical Datum of 1988. Modern datum offset approximately −3.18 ft from NGVD29 at the Saltair gauge. Always verify which datum is in use when comparing numbers.
ΔV (Delta-V)
Annual change in lake volume (kAf/yr). Positive = net gain; negative = net loss. The “vital sign” tracked by the simulator.

Climate & Teleconnection

PDO (Pacific Decadal Oscillation)
A pattern of Pacific Ocean sea-surface temperature variability on decadal timescales. Used in the simulator as a regime indicator (not a direct driver) that the GSL system tracks with ~8-year lag.
ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation)
Interannual climate pattern driven by tropical Pacific SST. El Niño (warm phase) tends to increase Utah precipitation; La Niña (cool phase) reduces it. Modifies the RC model’s effective time constant.
Teleconnection
A statistical or physical relationship between climate variables at widely separated geographic locations. The PDO–GSL link is a teleconnection: Pacific Ocean conditions influence Great Basin weather with a cumulative ~8-year lag.
ET₀ (Reference Evapotranspiration)
The rate of evapotranspiration from a standardized grass reference surface. Used as a proxy for atmospheric evaporative demand. Computed via the Hargreaves or Penman-Monteith equation.
CFSv2
Climate Forecast System version 2 (NCEP). A coupled atmosphere-ocean-land model providing seasonal forecasts up to 9 months ahead. Referenced for context; the v10.4 climate adjustment uses observed ET₀ vs. monthly climatology rather than CFSv2 seasonal forecasts.

Statistical Terms

Pearson r (Correlation Coefficient)
A measure of linear association between two variables, ranging from −1 (perfect negative) through 0 (none) to +1 (perfect positive). In the GSL context, raw Pearson r between PDO and lake volume at 8-year lag is ~0.15 — modest because interannual noise obscures the decadal signal.
R² (Coefficient of Determination)
The fraction of variance in one variable explained by another. R² = r². An R² of 0.55 means 55% of the year-to-year variation in ΔV is explained by the comparison dataset.
p-value
The probability that the observed correlation (or a stronger one) would arise by chance if no real relationship existed. A p-value < 0.001 means there is less than a 0.1% chance the PDO–GSL teleconnection is a statistical fluke.
Bandpass Filtering
A signal-processing technique that isolates oscillations within a specified frequency range (here, 8–25 year periods), removing both high-frequency noise (weather) and long-term trends (climate change, diversions) to reveal the underlying decadal coherence.
Cross-Correlation
A measure of similarity between two time series as a function of a time lag applied to one of them. The PDO–GSL cross-correlation peaks at ~8 years, meaning today’s Pacific conditions best predict lake conditions 8 years hence.
Coherence (Spectral)
The frequency-domain equivalent of correlation. High coherence at a specific period (e.g., 8–25 years) means two signals share a strong, consistent relationship at that timescale, even if their broadband correlation is low.

Model & Engineering

RC Circuit
Resistor-Capacitor circuit. The electrical analogue at the heart of this simulator: water volume = charge, elevation = voltage, flow = current, storage = capacitance, losses = resistance.
Damped Oscillator
A system that oscillates with decreasing amplitude over time. GSL’s post-1977 volume oscillations exhibit exponential damping — each successive swing is weaker, indicating loss of hydrologic resilience.
Q-Factor (Quality Factor)
In engineering, the ratio of energy stored to energy lost per cycle. A declining Q-factor for GSL means the lake loses more water (through diversions and evaporation) relative to its natural recharge with each oscillation cycle.
τ (Tau — Time Constant)
The characteristic response time of an RC stage. τfast = 1.5 yr (surface runoff), τslow = 20 yr (groundwater baseflow). Together they determine how quickly the watershed responds to climate forcing.
LEP (Lake-Effect Precipitation)
Precipitation generated when cold air passes over the relatively warm lake surface, creating snowfall downwind. A positive feedback: higher lake → larger surface area → more LEP → more inflow.
k-NN Resampling
k-Nearest Neighbor resampling. The statistical method used by Tarboton (2025) to generate 1,000 stochastic future climate sequences by drawing from the historical record. Produces the probability envelope shown in the simulator.

Organizations & Programs

GSL (Great Salt Lake)
The largest saltwater lake in the Western Hemisphere, located in northern Utah. Terminal (endorheic) basin with no outlet — water leaves only by evaporation.
GSR (Great Salt River Initiative)
A proposed large-scale western water augmentation project with Phase 0 (urgent and aggressive conservation, ~800 kAf/yr); Phase I: rescuing winter vegetable farms in Imperial, Yuma and Mexicali valleys; and Phase III: new water infrastructure to GSL, ~1,200 kAf/yr. Target: restore GSL to 4,207 ft NGVD29, as well as bringing new water to the CO River, to the Wasatch and to the I-15 corridor. Visit www.GreatSaltRiver.org.
GSLST (Great Salt Lake Strike Team)
Utah Governor’s task force producing the 2026 Data & Insights Summary report. Identifies the 800 kAf/yr structural deficit and the seven policy intervention categories.
USGS
United States Geological Survey. Operates the Saltair gage (10010000) providing continuous lake elevation data since 1903. Also publishes bathymetric maps.
