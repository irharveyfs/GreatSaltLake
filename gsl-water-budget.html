⚠️ Data Anomaly and Methodology Shift (1983–Present)

1983–1986 Wet Cycle vs. Post‑1987 Residual Inflow

The original static charts showed an unusually smooth decline in river inflow after 1984, with far fewer peaks and valleys than before 1983. This version corrects that by using the Net_Budget_kAF_CORRECTED series from the 1930–2024 CSV and plotting all components directly from the same dataset. [file:5]

From 1949–1986, river inflows are primarily based on USGS gaging station measurements and regression infilling (Loving et al. 2000; Mohammed & Tarboton 2012). After gage closures and data limitations in the mid‑1980s, Tarboton (2022) extends inflows using residual water‑balance methods tied to lake elevation change, evaporation, and diversions. [file:3][file:5]

This methodology change explains why post‑1984 river inflow appears smoother and less granular than earlier periods: inflows are increasingly constrained by the mass balance and lake elevation record rather than direct daily gage variability, even though the net budget series continues to show quasi‑decadal oscillations on top of a long‑term deficit. [file:3][file:4][file:5]

Key point: The 1983–1986 wet cycle is a real climate signal validated by streamflow and paleoclimate reconstructions, while the reduced variability in post‑1987 inflows reflects a shift from predominantly measured to more residually derived inflow estimates, not a coding error in this explorer. [file:3][file:4][file:5]

Streamflow Data Sources & Methodology

Inflow estimates derive from USGS gaging stations, regression relationships, and residual water‑balance closure, following Loving et al. (2000), Mohammed & Tarboton (2012), and Tarboton (2022). [file:3][file:5]

Primary Gage: USGS 10127110 – Bear River Basin Outflow at Highway 83 near Corinne, UT (1971–1986; used with regression to extend 1949–present). [file:3]
Regression Infilling: Bear River at Corinne 10126000 from Bear River near Collinston 10118000 using:
bcor = 1.099522 × bcol (Adj R² ≈ 0.994). [file:3]
Highway Extension: bhwy = 14,256.223 + 1.006971 × bcor (Adj R² ≈ 0.998), representing additional flow crossing Highway 83 that bypasses 10126000 as documented in Waddell & Fields (1977). [file:3]
Long‑Term Baseline: USGS 10118000 – Bear River near Collinston (1902–present) provides the long historical context and supports regression robustness. [file:3]
Primary Gage: USGS 10141000 – Weber River near Plain City, UT (1907–present). [file:3]
Secondary Sloughs: Hooper Slough 10141040 and Howard Slough 10141400 are infilled with regressions of the form:
hooper = 0.0062 × weber + 8,704
howard = 0.0171 × weber + 12,985 [file:3]
Methodological Note: Despite scatter and limited overlap, these relationships follow Loving et al. (2000) to maintain consistency with USGS Water‑Resources Investigations Report 00‑4221. [file:3]
Primary Control: USGS 10143500 – Centerville Creek above Diversions, infilled using Red Butte Creek 10172200 via:
center = 0.775 × red (Adj R² ≈ 0.886). [file:3]
Secondary Creeks: Holmes, Ricks, Parrish, Stone, and Mill Creeks are regressed on Centerville Creek with slopes between about 0.72 and 3.00 and R² ≈ 0.95–0.96, preserving relative scaling among small basins. [file:3]
Primary Flow: Combined Jordan River and Surplus Canal at Salt Lake City, USGS 10170490, treated as the main Jordan inflow. [file:3]
City Creek: USGS 10172500 – City Creek near Salt Lake City, with missing periods filled from Farmington Creek using:
city = 0.637 × farmington (Adj R² ≈ 0.74–0.74). [file:3]
Industrial and Minor Tributaries: Lee Creek 10172640 and Kennecott Drain 10172650 are extended using seasonal patterns derived from Loving et al. (2000) and reconciled with DWR records where possible. [file:3]

Other Budget Components

Evaporation (Corrected vs. Original)

Evaporation is a dominant outflow and historically the largest source of uncertainty. The CSV distinguishes between original pan‑based estimates and later temperature–salinity corrections. [file:3][file:5]

Component Method Usage in This Explorer
Evaporation_ORIGINAL Pan evaporation and empirical adjustments, known to be biased low before the 1980s. Used for historical comparison only; not plotted as the main evaporation series.
Evaporation_CORRECTED Physically based model depending on air temperature and brine salinity (Mohammed & Tarboton 2012). Plotted here (negative) as the primary evaporation series used for net budget closure.

Analyses suggest that earlier evaporation estimates underestimated losses by roughly 15–25% on average, which can explain part of the apparent pre‑1980 water‑budget imbalance without invoking unrealistic inflow spikes. [file:3][file:4]

Water Diversions and Extractions

The CSV aggregates traditional diversions, mountain aqueducts, and mineral extraction to produce a combined TotalDiversions_kAF series used in this chart. [file:5]

Long‑term records from Utah Division of Water Resources show growth from early 20th‑century diversions below 500 kAF/yr to modern combined withdrawals well above 2,000 kAF/yr when agricultural, municipal, and industrial uses are included. [file:3][file:5]

Net Budget and Post‑1984 Trend

Corrected Net Budget (1930–2024)

The black line in the chart uses the Net_Budget_kAF_CORRECTED field directly from the CSV rather than being recalculated in the browser, ensuring consistency with the underlying hydrologic analysis. [file:5]

This corrected net budget shows large positive surpluses during the 1983–1986 wet cycle followed by a persistent negative regime post‑1987, consistent with published work linking structural deficits to increased diversions and a drier climate baseline rather than to graphical artifacts. Data Quality Notes

High Confidence (≈1949–2008)

Period with dense USGS gaging, overlapping regressions, and detailed cooperative investigations; both inflows and evaporation are well constrained by measurements and physically based models. [file:3][file:5]

Medium Confidence (1930–1948)

Earlier records rely on sparser gage data, back‑extrapolated regressions, and less certain evaporation estimates; still useful for context but best treated cautiously in trend attribution. [file:3][file:5]

Medium Confidence (2017–2024)

Latest years extend regressions and residual methods into a changing measurement environment; comparison with DWR diversion records and independent lake‑level data is recommended for critical applications. [file:3][file:5]
